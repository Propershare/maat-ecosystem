THE ARAB CONQUEST OF KMT (639–700 CE)

I. Loss of Indigenous Kemitan Autonomy (Before 639 CE)
- Religious bans: hieroglyphic, priestly, and temple-based Kemetic traditions effectively criminalized.
- Intense taxation, religious persecution of Coptic Christians (indigenous Kmtians), and political instability.
- Sasanian war devastates Kmt: land, irrigation, and population collapse set the stage.
- Empire is: exhausted, unpopular, and economically fragile—making Kmt vulnerable.

II. Arab Invasion (639–640 CE)
- Arabs receive approval from Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab to invade Kmt.
- Sinai invaded with ~3,500–4,000 soldiers, later reinforced to about 8,000–10,000.
- Minimal resistance posed due to low Byzantine military presence.
- Arabs seize towns and fortresses without major resistance.

III. Military Campaigns (640–642 CE)
- Capture of Kmt's eastern gateway: Pelusium.
- Rapid invasion westward to Bilbeis; Bilbeis falls.
- Coptic populations remain non-supportive of the Byzantines.
- Arabs seize the key Byzantine fortress controlling Upper and Lower Kmt (Old Cairo).
- Extended siege begins.
- Fall of the field-force near Heliopolis (Iunu), one of the oldest sacred cities of ancient Kmt.
- Byzantine Kmt is shattered.

IV. Final Capitulation (642 CE)
- Alexandria surrenders to Arab forces—decisive military event of the conquest.
- Byzantines formally withdraw.
- Caliph’s representative signs treaty with the Byzantine governor, Cyrus (also the Melkite Patriarch).
- Treaty grants security of persons and property, allowing indigenous Kmtians to pay jizya (head tax).
- Marks the end of Byzantine rule.

V. Consolidation (643–644 CE)
- Amr ibn al-As establishes Fustat, the first Arab-capital in Kmt, outside Babylon.
- Moves political heart of Kmt from Mediterranean/African orientation to Arab center.

VI. Arab Reforms (643–644 CE)
- Land and taxation reform.
- Land surveys begin; Arab administrators assume control of agriculture, irrigation, and taxation.
- Indigenous Kmtian populations relegated to peasants and taxpayers.

VII. Rebellions & Reassertion of Control (644–666 CE)
- Sporadic rebellions by Kmtian peasants and remnants of Byzantine factions suppressed.
- Arab garrisons expand throughout the Nile Valley.

VIII. Arab Domination (666–700 CE)
- Kmt becomes a stable province of the Rashidun and then Umayyad Caliphates.

IX. Arabization of KMT (650–800 CE)
- Arabic gradually replaces Greek and Coptic in administration.
- Arab settlers receive land-grants; new tribal communities established.
- 674–750: Shift in elite identity.
- Intermarriage; privileges for Arab descent; military hierarchies elevated.
- Indigenous populations adopt Arab names and speak Arabic for socioeconomic access.
- 700 CE: Arabic becomes administrative language.
- Caliph Abd al-Malik orders all government records to be kept in Arabic.
- Coptic literacy declines; African memory-systems weakened.

X. Islamization of KMT (640–900 CE)
- Conversion to Islam initially low; indigenous Kmtians primarily Christian (Coptic).
- Economic pressures: taxation, land seizures, jizya power-converts.
- Caliphs promote conversions; property transfer to Muslims—accelerating conversion.
- Mass conversions ~800–900 CE: Kmtian population becomes majority Muslim.

XI. Long-Term Consequences (661 CE–Present)
1. Disappearance of Indigenous Languages
- Kmtic and Coptic lost as dominant languages by 1100s.
- Arabic replaces native linguistic heritage.

2. Erasure of Kemetic Historical Identity
- Destruction of archives, libraries, temples.
- Loss of native script, science, priesthood, and historical memory.

3. Subordination in Islamic Order
- Indigenous Africans subordinated within Arab-Muslim hierarchy.
- Arab descent favored; native identities suppressed.

4. Persistence of Coptic Resistance
- Coptic Christians maintain memory of Kemetic heritage.
- Face persecution, exclusion, and marginalization.

5. Modern Legacies
- Arabicized identity dominates postcolonial "Egypt."
- Loss of connection to African-Kemetic civilizational origins.
